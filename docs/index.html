0Numeraire = the unit you measure prices in

Tiny example

A laptop costs ₹60,000. It also costs $720. Same laptop, two answers. Why? Because we measured it in two different units.

Unit usedLaptop pricePrice of the unit itself
Rupee60,000₹1 = 1
Dollar720$1 = 1

The exchange rate ₹/$ is just the laptop-of-currencies ratio: 60,000 ÷ 720 ≈ 83.33. Notice: whichever unit you pick, it always costs exactly one of itself.

Plain words

A numeraire is simply the unit of measurement for prices — the "one rupee" or "one dollar" you quote everything against. In finance the unit can be a bond, a stock, or even a whole portfolio. Swap the unit and every price changes… but the laptop doesn't get more or less valuable.

Formula (now it's obvious)

If $N$ is the numeraire, every asset price $P$ is quoted as $P/N$, and $N/N = 1$ — the numeraire's own price is always 1.

Common confusion

"Numeraire sounds like a security type." No — it's a ruler, not a thing being measured. Cash/bonds are just the rulers everyone defaults to.

Self-check — Ch.0
Q1. If gold is priced per ounce and per gram, is the ounce price "more real"?
Show answer

No. Both describe the same object; the unit is arbitrary. That's the whole numeraire idea.

Q2. What is the price of the numeraire in itself?
Show answer

Always 1. ₹ measured in rupees: 1. A stock measured in shares of itself: 1.

Q3. Laptop ₹60,000 = $720. Implied ₹/$?
Show answer

60,000 / 720 ≈ 83.33.

Further reading: any intro FX/textbook chapter on "units of account"; McNeil/Frey notes on numeraire pricing, ch. 1 (read for the rupee-dollar framing, skip the proofs).

1Risk-neutral probability — a price-implied fake chance

Tiny example

Stock A costs 100 today. In one year it is 150 or 50. Bank interest $r = 0$ (money just sits there).

Ask: what fake probability $q$ makes "average tomorrow price" equal today's price?

$$100 = q \cdot 150 + (1-q) \cdot 50 \;\Rightarrow\; 100 = 50 + 100q \;\Rightarrow\; q = 0.5$$

Check: average future price $= 0.5 \times 150 + 0.5 \times 50 = 100$ ✔ equals today's price.

Now Stock B: 100 → 130 or 80:

$$100 = q \cdot 130 + (1-q) \cdot 80 \;\Rightarrow\; 100 = 80 + 50q \;\Rightarrow\; q = 0.4$$

Check: $0.4 \times 130 + 0.6 \times 80 = 52 + 48 = 100$ ✔

Plain words

A risk-neutral probability is the made-up chance that makes a stock's expected future price equal its current price once you account for interest. It is not anyone's real belief about the world. Your uncle may swear the stock jumps to 150 with 90% chance — the market price still says q = 0.5. Two stocks gave different q's (0.5 vs 0.4) from nothing but their own prices: q is a property of pricing, not of opinion.

And this fake-probability world has a name: a measure — just a complete list of chances assigned to every scenario, that adds up to 1. The real-world list is one measure; the price-implied list (q) is another.

Formula (with interest)

When $r \neq 0$, money grows, so compare against the forward price $S_0 e^{rT}$:

$$q = \frac{S_0 e^{rT} - S_{down}}{S_{up} - S_{down}}$$

Same Stock A with r = 5%: $q = (100e^{0.05} - 50)/100 ≈ 0.551$. Higher interest → higher q.

Common confusion

"Risk-neutral means investors don't like risk." Opposite-ish: it's a bookkeeping trick. Pricing under these fake chances gives the same answer as pricing under real chances with a risk premium baked in — because both must reproduce today's market price.

Self-check — Ch.1
Q1. Stock 100 → 120 or 70, r = 0. Find q.
Show answer

100 = q·120 + (1−q)·70 → 100 = 70 + 50q → q = 0.6.

Q2. Does q change if traders become very optimistic about the up move?
Show answer

Only if today's price changes. Optimism moves prices, and then q recomputes. q tracks prices, not feelings.

Q3. r rises, everything else fixed — does q rise or fall?
Show answer

Rise: the required average future price $S_0e^{rT}$ goes up, so more weight must sit on the up branch.

Further reading: Shreve, Stochastic Calculus for Finance II ch. 3 (one-period model); Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (1979) original paper §I.

2Binomial vs Black–Scholes: same idea, more steps

Tiny example

Price the call (strike 100) on Stock A from Ch.1: payoff is max(S−100, 0) → 50 in up state, 0 in down. Under q = 0.5: price = 0.5 × 50 + 0.5 × 0 = 25.

Now chop the year into more steps (each step: ±20%-ish). Each finer tree re-prices the same way — fake chances at every node, average, roll back. With σ = 20%, the numbers creep toward Black–Scholes:

Plain words

Binomial = discrete time: a few scenarios, easy to hand-check. Black–Scholes = continuous time: infinitely many tiny steps, so you need calculus instead of a spreadsheet. Nothing magical happens in the limit — the same "average under fake chances" rule survives. What carries over: fake probabilities, no-arbitrage pricing, the rollback logic. What's new: you can no longer list scenarios; they become a smooth distribution (the bell curve of Ch.6).

Formula

CRR tree step sizes: $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\; d = 1/u,\; p = \dfrac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d}$ — identical shape to Ch.1's $q$, just per-step.

Common confusion

"Black–Scholes is a different theory than the binomial model." It's the same pricing rule viewed through infinitely many steps. Students who treat them as rivals memorize twice as much for half the understanding.

Self-check — Ch.2
Q1. One-step tree, S = 100 → 110/90, r = 0, strike 100 call. Price?
Show answer

q = (100−90)/(110−90) = 0.5; payoff 10/0 → price 5.

Q2. Doubling tree steps changes the pricing rule or only its resolution?
Show answer

Resolution. Rule stays: fake-chance average, rolled back step by step.

Q3. Why do we bother with continuous time at all?
Show answer

Closed-form answers, Greeks, and tractable models — impossible to write down with 10²⁰ tree nodes.

Further reading: Hull, Options, Futures & Other Derivatives ch. 13 (binomial) → ch. 14 (BS) back-to-back; note how every discrete quantity keeps its meaning.

3A call option = receive the share, minus pay the strike

Tiny example

You hold the call on Stock A (strike 100). At expiry two worlds exist:

ScenarioYou receive (share)You pay (strike)Net payoff
Up (S = 150)15010050
Down (S = 50)— (walk away)00

Split the bookkeeping: receiving the share averages $0.5\times150 + 0.5\times0 = 75$... but you only hand over the 100 strike in the up world. Average strike payment $= 0.5 \times 100 + 0.5 \times 0 = 50$. Net value $= 75 - 50 = 25$ ✔ matches Ch.2.

The second term — "you pay the strike only in the scenarios where you end up in the money" — is worth $100 \times (\text{fake chance of ending ITM}) = 100 \times 0.5 = 50$. That bracket is the seed of $N(d_2)$.

Plain words

A call isn't one product, it's two legs: get the stock (but only sometimes), pay the strike (but only sometimes). Once you see the "pay 100 only in the winning scenarios" leg, Black–Scholes stops being a spell: $Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$ is literally "the strike cash, times the fake chance you actually have to pay it."

Formula (preview)

$$C = \underbrace{S_0 N(d_1)}_{\text{shares received, priced}} - \underbrace{K e^{-rT} N(d_2)}_{\text{strike paid, discounted}}$$

Ch.6 shows why the share leg uses $N(d_1)$ while the strike leg uses $N(d_2)$ — it's the numeraire swap from Ch.4 hiding inside the bell curve.

Common confusion

"The option 'is' the stock." Careful: you receive the share only in ITM scenarios. The probability weighting is the whole point — an option is a conditional claim.

Self-check — Ch.3
Q1. Strike 100, fake chance of finishing ITM = 0.3, r = 0. Value of the "pay strike" leg?
Show answer

100 × 0.3 = 30.

Q2. Deep in-the-money call (S ≫ K): which leg dominates?
Show answer

Both nearly certain: C ≈ S − Ke^{−rT}, i.e. stock leg minus almost-full strike leg (N→1).

Q3. Deep out-of-the-money: what happens to the strike leg?
Show answer

Its chance collapses to ~0 — you almost never pay the strike, so the option is nearly worthless.

Further reading: Taleb, Dynamic Hedging opening chapters on call decomposition; Bergman et al., "Portfolio replication with options" (conceptual, skim).

4Change of numeraire, in discrete time: reweight by growth

Tiny example — two states

Back to Stock A (100 → 150/50) with bank chances $(0.5,\;0.5)$. Now measure everything in shares of the stock instead of rupees. Rule: multiply each scenario's chance by $\dfrac{\text{stock price tomorrow}}{\text{stock price today}}$:

up: $0.5 \times \frac{150}{100} = 0.5 \times 1.5 = 0.75$,   down: $0.5 \times \frac{50}{100} = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$. Sum = 1 ✔

Why that's sensible: the stock ends the year worth 150 in the up world, 50 in the down world. Of its total 200 "tomorrow-value", 150/200 = 75% sits in the up scenario. If your ruler is the stock, scenarios where the ruler grew big automatically count bigger. Plain-words version: ₹75 of the share's ₹100 expected value sits in the up scenario — wait, expected value is exactly 100 (Ch.1!), so the up branch holds 75% of it. Reweighting just reads off that split.

Tiny example — three states

Bank chances $(25, 50, 25)\%$ over stock prices $(50, 100, 150)$. Check: average $= 0.25(50)+0.5(100)+0.25(150) = 100$ ✔ (so these were already risk-neutral chances).

Reweight each by price ÷ 100: $(0.25{\times}0.5,\; 0.5{\times}1.0,\; 0.25{\times}1.5) = (0.125,\; 0.5,\; 0.375)$ — i.e. 12.5 / 50 / 37.5, sums to 1 ✔.

New average stock price $= 0.125(50) + 0.5(100) + 0.375(150) = 6.25 + 50 + 56.25 = \mathbf{112.5}$. Possible prices unchanged (still 50/100/150) — only the weights moved. Measured in shares, the stock now drifts upward: the share ruler was flat in rupee terms but "shrinks" relative to its own good scenarios.

Tiny example — Stock B

B: 100 → 130/80, bank q = 0.4. Switch to share ruler: $0.4 \times \frac{130}{100} = \mathbf{0.52}$, down: $0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.48$. Sum 1 ✔.

Plain words

Changing numeraire = changing the camera angle. Rupee-camera: stock's average tomorrow = 100 (flat). Share-camera: the ruler itself stretches in up-worlds, so those worlds get extra weight. A martingale is just "expected tomorrow value = today's value" — the stock is a martingale under the bank ruler, and becomes non-flat under the share ruler (while other things, like bond prices in shares, become flat instead).

Formula

For any numeraire $N$ (today $N_0$): $$\pi_i^{N} = \frac{p_i \, N_i(T)}{N_0 \sum_j p_j N_j(T)} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{when } E[N_T]=N_0}\quad \pi_i^{N} = p_i \frac{N_i(T)}{N_0}$$

The examples above used the shortcut because risk-neutral chances already make the numerator average to $N_0$.

Common confusion

"Reweighting changed the possible outcomes." It didn't — 150 is still 150. Only the weights changed. Prices of assets in each scenario are facts of the world; chances are choices of ruler.

Self-check — Ch.4
Q1. Bank chances (0.5, 0.5), stock 100 → 200/0. Share-ruler chances?
Show answer

up: 0.5×200/100 = 1, down: 0.5×0/100 = 0. All weight on up — degenerate ruler (stock can vanish!); in practice require N > 0.

Q2. Three-state example: what did the average stock price become under the share ruler, and why isn't it 100 anymore?
Show answer

112.5. Flatness held for the stock under the bank ruler; measuring in the stock itself breaks its own symmetry — up-states are where the ruler is generous.

Q3. Stock B share-ruler up-chance computed above?
Show answer

0.52.

Further reading: Brigo & Mercurio, Interest Rate Models ch. 3 §3.1 (change of numeraire, discrete appendix); Björk, Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time ch. 10 intro pages.

5Price invariance: the option costs the same in rupees or in shares

Tiny example — the same call, two ways

Call on Stock B (100 → 130/80, strike 100). Payoffs: up = 30, down = 0.

Rupee ruler: $0.4 \times 30 + 0.6 \times 0 = \mathbf{12}$.

Share ruler: express payoffs in shares: up pays $30/130$ shares, down pays $0/80$ shares. Weight by share-ruler chances (0.52, 0.48), then convert back to rupees at today's share price (100):

$$\frac{30}{130} \times 0.52 \times 100 + \frac{0}{80} \times 0.48 \times 100 = 0.2308 \times 52 = \mathbf{12}$$

Watch the cancellation: the 0.52 carried a factor $130/100$ from Ch.4, and the payoff carried $1/130$. Multiply: $\frac{130}{100}\times\frac{30}{130} = \frac{30}{100}$ — the 130 vanishes. Probabilities and units cancel; the price survives.

Plain words

Changing units can't change what something costs — you can't make a laptop more valuable by switching to dollars. Same here: any valid ruler prices any contract identically. This is why "choose the convenient numeraire" is a superpower in interest-rate derivatives (Ch.8): pick the ruler that makes the math collapse, trust the price is unchanged.

Formula

For any two numeraires $A, B$: $$\frac{\text{Price}}{A_0} = E^{A}\!\left[\frac{X_T}{A_T}\right] = \frac{B_0}{A_0}\, E^{B}\!\left[\frac{X_T}{B_T}\right]$$ — one identity, two rulers, same rupee answer.

Common confusion

"Different chances ⇒ different price." Different chances plus different units ⇒ same price. If you reweight chances but keep quoting payoffs in rupees, you'll wrongly think the price moved.

Self-check — Ch.5
Q1. Put on B (payoff 20 in down state). Rupee-ruler price?
Show answer

0.6 × 20 = 12. (Share ruler: 20/80 × 0.48 × 100 = 12 ✔.)

Q2. Which factors cancelled to make both methods agree?
Show answer

The reweighting factor $S_{up}/S_0$ and the payoff conversion $1/S_{up}$ — the scenario price appeared once up (in chances) once down (in units) and dropped out.

Q3. Someone gets 13 via the share method. Most likely bug?
Show answer

They converted payoffs to shares but forgot to convert the final answer back (multiply by $S_0$), or double-counted the 130.

Further reading: Musiela & Rutkowski, Martingale Methods in Financial Modelling ch. 9 §1 (numeraire invariance, discrete preview).

6N(d₂) is an area; N(d₁) is the same event under the share ruler

Tiny example

$S=100, K=100, r=0, \sigma=0.2, T=1$. Then

$$d_2 = \frac{\ln(S/K) - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} = \frac{0 - 0.02}{0.2} = -0.1,\qquad d_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{T} = -0.1 + 0.2 = 0.1$$

$N(d_2) = N(-0.1) \approx 0.46$, $N(d_1) = N(0.1) \approx 0.54$, and $C = 100(0.54) - 100(0.46) = \mathbf{7.96}$.

More in-the-money ($S=120$): $N(d_2) \approx 0.79$, $N(d_1) \approx 0.84$. They move together, and nothing forces them to sum to 1 (they're areas under two different curves).

Plain words

Tomorrow's log-price is a bell curve. "$N(d_2)$" = the shaded area to the right of the strike line — the bank-ruler chance you finish in the money (compare: Ch.3's "pay strike only in ITM worlds"). Now recall Ch.4: switching to the share ruler reweights each scenario by its growth. For a bell curve, multiplying by "growth" is exactly sliding the whole bell to the right — the centre moves from $(r-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})T$ to $(r+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})T$, a shift of $\sigma^2T$, which in standardised units is $\sigma\sqrt{T}$. Same event ("finish ITM"), new ruler ⇒ area $N(d_1)$. And that's why $d_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{T}$: it is the slide.

So Black–Scholes = Ch.3's two legs, each measured in its natural ruler: shares-received valued under the share ruler ($N(d_1)$), strike-paid valued under the rupee ruler ($N(d_2)$).

Formula

$$d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)$$

Common confusion

"N(d₁) is the probability of exercising and N(d₂) is something else too — maybe they should add to 1?" Neither is a plain exercise probability alone: N(d₂) is the ITM chance; N(d₁) is that same chance measured in share-units (a share-weighted chance). Areas under different curves needn't sum to anything.

Self-check — Ch.6
Q1. σ↑ with everything else fixed: does the slide σ√T grow or shrink?
Show answer

Grow — the two bell curves separate more, so N(d₁) − N(d₂) widens (option value rises with vol).

Q2. ATM (S=K, r=0): d₂ = −σ²T/2/(σ√T) = −σ√T/2. With σ=0.2,T=1?
Show answer

d₂ = −0.1, matching the example.

Q3. Why does the deep-ITM case give N(d₁)≈N(d₂)≈1 and C≈S−K?
Show answer

Both bells put ~all area right of the line; both rulers agree you'll surely receive the share and pay the strike — deterministic payoff, no optionality left.

Further reading: Lewis, The Psychology of Tech Analysis-free Option Pricing ch. 3 (the cleanest derivation of the two-numeraire split); Spreadr/QuantStart visual essays on N(d1)/N(d2) — read after this chapter, not before.

7Girsanov: the reweighting machine, discretely then continuously

Stage 1 — discrete (you've done this already)

Stock A, bank chances (0.5, 0.5) over (150, 50): average = 100. Switch to share ruler (Ch.4): chances (0.75, 0.25). Possible prices unchanged — 150 and 50 are still the only futures. But the average moves: $0.75 \times 150 + 0.25 \times 50 = \mathbf{125}$. Reweighting kept the world intact and moved the drift.

Plain words

Girsanov's theorem is the continuous-time version of exactly that trick: if you reweight every path by a positive factor that averages to 1, the randomness (volatility) survives untouched and only the drift shifts. "Reweight paths" is Ch.4's "reweight scenarios" with infinitely many steps. When the stock is the new ruler, the Brownian motion's drift shifts from $r$ to $r + \sigma^2$ — the bell-slide of Ch.6 in differential clothing.

Formula

If $\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^S}{d\mathbb{Q}} = \frac{S_T/S_0}{E[S_T/S_0]} = e^{\sigma W_T - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T}$ (the reweighting factor), then under the share ruler $W_t^{S} = W_t - \sigma t$ is a fresh Brownian motion and

$$\frac{dS}{S} = r\,dt + \sigma\,dW \;=\; (r + \sigma^2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW^{S}.$$

Same $\sigma$ (randomness preserved), drift $r \to r+\sigma^2$. Integrate the drift gap over $[0,T]$ and standardise by $\sigma\sqrt{T}$: that's $d_1 - d_2 = \sigma\sqrt{T}$ again.

Common confusion

"Girsanov changes the noise." It doesn't — volatility is invariant; only drift moves. If your transformed process had a different σ, you reweighted wrong (the factor wasn't mean-1).

Self-check — Ch.7
Q1. Discrete stage: what stayed fixed and what moved when we switched rulers?
Show answer

Scenario prices fixed (150/50); chances and therefore the average (100→125) moved.

Q2. Drift under the share ruler in the formula above?
Show answer

$r + \sigma^2$.

Q3. Where did $d_1 - d_2 = \sigma\sqrt{T}$ reappear?
Show answer

As the integrated drift shift of Ch.6's bell slide — Girsanov is the engine behind the N(d₁)/N(d₂) gap.

Further reading: Shreve ch. 9 (Girsanov carefully, after a probability refresher); Lipton, Asset Paths ch. 3 for the measure-change zoo in one place.

8Preview: the forward measure (stub)

Where this goes next

Pick a zero-coupon bond maturing at T as the ruler instead of cash or stock, and the same reweighting machinery produces the forward measure: under it, the T-forward price of anything is automatically flat (a martingale), so pricing a payoff at T is just "average it, no discounting dance". This single choice is the backbone of interest-rate modelling: Vasicek and Hull–White under the T-bond ruler, HJM's drift condition, and the LIBOR market model's tenor-by-tenor measure switches. All of it is Ch.4 with bonds as the camera. Next guide in this series: "Swaptions and the Forward Measure, Example First."

Common confusion

"Forward measure ≠ risk-neutral measure, so prices differ." Rulers differ, prices don't (Ch.5). The forward measure just deletes exponential-discounting algebra from every expectation.

Self-check — Ch.8
Q1. Numeraire for the forward measure at T?
Show answer

The zero-coupon bond P(t,T).

Q2. What becomes flat under it?
Show answer

Any T-payoff divided by the bond — i.e. forward prices.

Q3. Which models live here?
Show answer

Vasicek, Hull–White, HJM, LMM — all exploit T-bond-numeraire flatness.

Further reading: Brigo–Mercurio ch. 3 (forward measure done gently); White (1980); Jamshidian (1997) for LMM motivation.
